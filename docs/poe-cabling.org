#+title:     PoE Cabling
#+language:  en

* The short version

*Cat5e or better.  That is the whole rule.*

If the jacket says =CAT5E=, =CAT6=, or =CAT6A=, the cable will carry any PoE
class we are likely to deploy — 802.3af, 802.3at, or 802.3bt — and there is
nothing further to check.  Buy on the category marking and move on.

* Why the category marking is sufficient

TIA-568 category compliance *requires solid copper conductors*.  Copper-clad
aluminium cannot legitimately be marked as Cat5e, because it cannot meet the
electrical requirements of the category.

So the category claim already subsumes the conductor question.  There is a lot
of writing on the internet about hunting for =CU= versus =CCA= markings on
cable jackets; treat that as a symptom of buying uncategorised bulk cable
rather than as a check we need to perform.  If we hold the Cat5e line, the CCA
question never comes up.

* Our standing assumption: minimum 1 Gb Ethernet

We hold the bar at *at least Cat5e everywhere*, and it is worth being explicit
that this is a *data* requirement, not a PoE one.

PoE is satisfied by a lower bar than gigabit is.  802.3at will run happily over
cable we would reject on throughput grounds.  Because our floor is 1 Gb
Ethernet, the cabling standard is set by the data side, and PoE suitability
comes along for free.

That ordering matters for how we reason about it: we are not choosing cable for
PoE and hoping it does gigabit.  We choose for gigabit, and PoE is never the
binding constraint.

Corollary: any cable good enough to be on this network is good enough to power
a device on this network.

* Reading a jacket

Markings repeat every foot or two, so a single glance may catch only part of
the legend.  Read along the cable before concluding something is missing.  A
typical print:

: BRANDNAME CAT6 U/UTP 23AWG 4PR TYPE CM (UL) c(UL) ... 305M

| marking            | meaning                                    | matters for PoE?              |
|--------------------+--------------------------------------------+-------------------------------|
| =CAT5E= / =CAT6=   | TIA-568 category                           | *yes — this is the one*       |
| =23AWG= / =24AWG=  | conductor gauge, lower is thicker          | only for thin patch cords     |
| =4PR=              | four twisted pairs                         | implied by the category       |
| =TYPE CM/CMR/CMP=  | NEC fire class — where you may run it      | no, it is a code question     |
| =(UL)= =c(UL)=     | safety listings, US and Canada             | no, but a provenance signal   |
| =CU= / =CCA=       | conductor material                         | moot if the category is real  |

* The one gauge caveat

24AWG is standard for solid horizontal cable and is entirely fine.

The exception is *slim or low-profile patch cords*, which are frequently 26AWG
or 28AWG.  These are legal and category-compliant, but their higher resistance
means reduced maximum length under PoE.  Fine for a short patch between a
switch and an injector; not what belongs in a permanent run.

For scale: 24AWG solid copper is roughly 0.084 Ω/m per conductor, giving about
8.4 Ω loop resistance over a full 100 m two-pair run, against the 12.5 Ω that
802.3at permits.  Comfortable margin, and every run we have is far shorter.

* Fire class — a location question, not a PoE question

Pick by where the cable runs, per NEC Article 800:

- =CM= — general purpose.  Rooms, surface runs, ordinary walls.
- =CMR= — riser.  Vertical runs passing between floors.
- =CMP= — plenum.  Spaces used for air handling, such as above a dropped
  ceiling that returns air.

This has no bearing on PoE capability.  It is about what the cable does in a
fire.

* When the category rule cannot be trusted

The rule fails only when the marking itself is untrustworthy:

- *No printing at all.*  Legitimate cable is marked.  Unmarked cable is the
  stuff you do not want carrying 30 W through a ceiling.
- *A category claim with no UL listing*, or a listing mark that looks
  inconsistent with the brand.
- *Bulk cable priced well below everything comparable.*  CCA is cheap for a
  reason.

If a spool is genuinely suspect, there is a definitive physical test: nick or
strip one conductor and look at the cut face.  Solid copper is copper coloured
all the way through; CCA shows a silver aluminium core beneath a thin copper
skin.  CCA is also noticeably stiffer and snaps after fewer bends.

* Pair count and PoE modes

802.3af and 802.3at deliver over two pairs; 802.3bt uses all four.  Any real
Cat5e has all eight conductors, so this is automatic.

The thing this rules out is cheap four-conductor "2-pair" cable sold for
100 Mbit runs — which also fails the gigabit floor, so the Cat5e rule catches
it before PoE ever enters the picture.

* Checklist

1. Does the jacket say Cat5e or better?  If yes, you are done.
2. Is it a slim patch cord?  Check for 26/28AWG and keep it short.
3. Does the run pass through a plenum or between floors?  Match the fire class.
4. No markings at all?  Do not use it.
